# Explaining Density: Why the Same Policy Works Differently in Two Neighbourhoods

## Abstract

This paper examines why an identical waste-sorting policy produced markedly different outcomes in two adjacent neighbourhoods. Drawing on six months of observation and fourteen interviews, it shows that explanation density—the number of face-to-face explanations per household per week—accounts for most of the variation. Income, housing type and facility provision are held roughly constant across the two cases. The paper contributes a middle-range concept that links policy design to street-level implementation, and it specifies the conditions under which the concept travels. It also argues that implementation research should treat explanation as an input rather than a residual.

Keywords: implementation; explanation density; waste sorting; neighbourhood governance; comparative case

## 1. Introduction

Two neighbourhoods, one policy, a threefold difference in outcome. Smith (2020) argues that such gaps are usually attributed to resident awareness. Yet the two cases compared here differ by less than five per cent on income, age structure and housing type. This paper asks what else could account for the gap, and it proposes a variable that sits between policy design and resident behaviour.

## 2. Framework

Recent work on platformisation and local governance has shown that implementation outcomes depend on the density of intermediation (Jones & Lee, 2021). Wang (2019) makes a similar point for public services, though without measuring the intermediation itself. Building on these accounts, this paper defines explanation density as the number of face-to-face explanations delivered per household per week. The concept is deliberately narrow: it captures who explains, how often, and to whom.

## 3. Evidence

In neighbourhood A, volunteers explained the sorting rules at the building entrance twice a day. In neighbourhood B, a weekly notice was posted in the lobby. Over six months, the sorting rate rose to 68 per cent in A and 23 per cent in B. Chen (2022) reports a comparable pattern in her study of visibility and place, which strengthens the plausibility of the mechanism.

## 4. Discussion

The finding matters for two reasons. First, it suggests that implementation research should treat explanation as an input rather than a residual category. Second, it qualifies claims that awareness campaigns are sufficient: in these cases, awareness was high in both neighbourhoods, but only one had sustained explanation. The concept does not travel to policies with low participation costs, where a single notice may suffice.
